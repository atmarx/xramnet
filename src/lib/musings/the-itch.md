The itch starts small.

I stand in the doorway, coffee in hand, halfway a grant proposal being described to me that I feel it.  "Can I buy a couple of hard drives and a little network storage device for the project?"  

I scratch it.  I send the email.  I build the spreadsheet.  I give them a number.  Everybody's happy.

Then it itches again.

---

Different faculty member.  Different doorway.  Same question wearing a different hat.  "What does Azure charge for..."  "Can we get a breakdown of..."  "The grant requires an itemized..."  And every time, I'm rebuilding the same answer from the same scattered pieces — a jigsaw puzzle I assemble every month only to dump back in the box.

I need to be clear about something: I love this work.  Working with researchers on their actual problems, figuring out what they need, navigating the weird intersection of academic bureaucracy and cloud computing — that's the good stuff.  The conversations are fascinating.  The projects are cool.  A biologist needs storage for a dataset that might change how we understand something.  A physicist needs GPU time to simulate something beautiful.  A grad student just needs someone to say "yes, we can do that, and here's what it'll cost."

I'm not complaining about *any* of that.

I'm complaining about the boilerplate.  The repetitive, soul-grinding, spreadsheet-wrangling, email-threading, format-converting work that sits between "what do you need?" and "here's what it costs."  The stuff that takes just as long the fiftieth time as it did the first, because nobody ever turned it into something reusable.  *That's* the itch.

---

There's a frog in a pot of water.  You know the one — the heat rises so gradually that the frog doesn't notice until it's too late.  It's a terrible metaphor, actually.  Real frogs jump out.  But we're not frogs, and the water we're sitting in isn't on a single burner.  It's heated from everywhere — the feeds, the news, the slow erosion of things you thought were settled.  And in the middle of all that, building a billing tool for research computing feels almost absurdly small.

I know.

But the big pots aren't mine to drain.  What I *can* do is look at the small pot I'm sitting in — the one where the same spreadsheet gets rebuilt every month, where the same questions get answered from scratch, where institutional knowledge lives in one person's head because there was never time to put it anywhere else — and decide to do something about *that* pot.

---

So I [built the thing](/projects/open-chargeback).  And I'm giving it away, because I know I'm not the only one sitting in warm water.

Every research computing group I've ever talked to has some version of this problem.  They're all building their own spreadsheets, their own workarounds, their own answers to the same questions.  And every hour they spend on that is an hour they're not spending on the part that actually matters — the part where a researcher walks into your office with an idea and you get to help them make it real.

That's the time I want back.  Not for me — for them.  For the person at another university who's the only one who knows how the billing works and is tired of being the bottleneck.  For anyone who'd rather spend their afternoon on the interesting problem than the boilerplate surrounding it.

The water's warm.  The itch is real.  Maybe the best I can do is scratch it, share what I built, and hope someone else gets an afternoon back.
